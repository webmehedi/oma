# LinkedIn — day 24

## Post

```
Most accounting software is built for a finance department.

If you are one person with six clients, you are not a finance department, and
that mismatch is not cosmetic.

It shows up as work. A chart of accounts you have to configure before you can
send anything. Draft, sent, viewed, partially paid, overdue — five states to
maintain when your actual question has two answers. Tax settings for
jurisdictions you don't operate in. An onboarding flow that assumes someone else
will be reconciling this, because in the world the software was designed for,
someone else does.

None of that is bad software. It's software solving a harder problem than the
one you have, and the price of the extra generality is paid in setup you do
alone, on an evening, before you've invoiced anybody.

The one-person version of this job is genuinely small. Who are my clients. What
did I bill them. Has it arrived. That's three questions, and a tool that answers
exactly those can be learned in a minute and re-learned in ten seconds after a
month away — which matters more than any feature, because if you invoice twice a
month you are always coming back cold.

I built for that shape deliberately, and the constraint I held was: if a feature
made the empty state harder to understand, it didn't go in.

The trade is real and I'll name it. Outgrow the small tool and you migrate. I'd
still take the version that's useful in the first ten minutes over the one
that's complete in the first ten hours.

Where's your line? At what point does a freelancer actually need the full suite?
```

## Before you publish

- Don't name a competitor. The post argues against a category and stays fair;
  naming one turns it into a comparison you'd then have to defend feature by
  feature, and you have not audited their product.

  post — several of them will be v1.1 scope.
